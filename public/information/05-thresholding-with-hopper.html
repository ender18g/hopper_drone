Turn light into a measurable decision

Binary vision and thresholding

Understand the exact brightness equation, calibrate a threshold from evidence, and build decisions that survive shadows, glare, and noise.

Lesson 05 of 9 24 min Vision foundations

Learning objectives

  1. Compute luminance from RGB pixels and map a percentage threshold to 0…255.
  2. Distinguish whole-frame coverage from a normalized X/Y pixel test.
  3. Calibrate with representative light and background conditions.
  4. Use repeated evidence or hysteresis instead of one brittle frame.
01 / 05

Every pixel becomes one bit

Thresholding compresses a color image into a binary image. The result is easy to reason about because every pixel is either white or black.

Y=0.2126R+0.7152G+0.0722BY = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B

Hopper Studio’s luminance calculation. Green contributes most because human visual sensitivity is strongest there.

B(x,y)={1,Y(x,y)≥2.55 τ0,Y(x,y)<2.55 τB(x,y) = \begin{cases} 1,&Y(x,y)\ge 2.55\,\tau\\ 0,&Y(x,y)<2.55\,\tau \end{cases}

τ is the displayed threshold percentage from 0 to 100. Multiplying by 2.55 converts it to an 8-bit cutoff from 0 to 255.

When invert is true, Hopper Studio swaps the white/black assignment after the comparison. It does not change the underlying brightness.

Threshold is not confidence

A threshold of 60% is a pixel-brightness cutoff. It is unrelated to the 55% confidence used by object detection.

02 / 05

Coverage asks a different question than center

white coverage=100 #{(x,y):B(x,y)=1}W H\text{white coverage} = 100\,\frac{\#\{(x,y):B(x,y)=1\}}{W\,H}

Full-frame coverage is the percentage of pixels classified as white.

Whole frame

seesBinary

Useful for large regions: a white landing sheet, dark doorway, or broad lighting change. It compares coverage with a minimum percentage.

Single coordinate

binaryAt

Checks one normalized X/Y pixel. The default 0,0 is the reticle; 100,100 is the top right. A one-pixel test is sensitive to noise and tiny shifts.

A center test can be true while total coverage is nearly zero. Conversely, a bright wall can dominate coverage even while the center points at a dark target.

Check your understanding · A white card covers 8% of the frame and the center reticle is on it. What can each test report?

binaryAt("white", 0, 0, 60) can be true. seesBinary("white", 60, false, 10) is false because 8% is below the 10% coverage requirement.

03 / 05

Calibrate from two distributions

  1. Connect the real camera in the room and lighting you will actually use.
  2. Collect brightness observations for the target and the background—not just one ideal frame.
  3. Choose a cutoff in the gap between the two distributions. If they overlap heavily, thresholding alone is the wrong tool.
  4. Test shadows, glare, oblique views, motion blur, and different distances.
  5. Choose a coverage threshold based on expected target size, then write it down with its unit.
SymptomLikely causeBetter response
Everything is whiteCutoff too low or strong glareRaise brightness threshold; diffuse the light.
Everything is blackCutoff too high or room too darkLower threshold; improve illumination.
Flickers between statesNoise near the cutoffRequire repeated hits or add hysteresis.
Works only at one distanceCoverage changes with apparent areaCalibrate size range or use center/alignment plus area.
White and gray overlapBrightness is not distinctive enoughUse color features, AprilTags, or a learned model.

05 / 05

Know when binary vision is enough

Good fit

Controlled contrast

A white card on a dark floor under stable indoor light is fast, explainable, and computationally cheap.

Weak fit

Semantic identity

Thresholding cannot tell a white mug from white paper. It measures brightness, not object category.

Weak fit

Changing illumination

Sunlight, automatic exposure, and shadows move the brightness distributions.

Alternative

Fiducial or learned vision

Use AprilTags for an engineered marker, COCO-SSD for known categories, or Teachable Machine for a custom whole-frame class.

Flight decision rule

Never make a high-consequence maneuver from one sampled pixel. Combine area, repeated frames, timeouts, and a safe fallback such as hover or land.
